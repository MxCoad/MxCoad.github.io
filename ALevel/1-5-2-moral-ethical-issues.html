Moral and ethical issues

The opportunities and risks that digital technology brings to individuals and society. These topics usually come up as extended-response questions, so the key skill is a balanced, applied discussion with a justified conclusion. Use the planner at the end to practise.

1.5.2a

Computers in the workforce

Opportunities

  • Automation takes on repetitive, dangerous or dirty jobs (robots in factories, mines, bomb disposal)
  • New jobs in technology: developers, technicians, data analysts
  • Greater productivity and accuracy; lower costs and prices
  • Remote and flexible working; global teams

Risks

  • Job losses, especially in manufacturing, retail checkouts, call centres and, increasingly, office work
  • Deskilling: skilled tasks reduced to supervising machines
  • New jobs need different skills, so those who lose jobs may not get them; retraining costs
  • Always-on culture, workplace stress; jobs moved overseas
1.5.2b

Automated decision making and artificial intelligence

Automated decision making means computers making decisions without a human: approving loans and insurance, sifting job applications, high-frequency stock trading, managing power grids. Artificial intelligence lets computers do tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising images and speech, translating, medical diagnosis, driving, writing text.

Opportunities

  • Fast and consistent: thousands of decisions a second, without tiredness or mood
  • Can spot patterns humans miss, e.g. early signs of disease in scans
  • Cheaper; available 24/7
  • Self-driving vehicles could reduce accidents caused by human error

Risks

  • Bias: a system trained on biased data repeats and amplifies it (e.g. against certain groups in hiring or policing)
  • Accountability: who is responsible when it goes wrong — the programmer, the company, the user?
  • Explainability: it may be impossible to explain why a decision was made
  • Errors can spread fast (e.g. a "flash crash" in stock trading); no common sense in unusual situations
  • Moral choices must be programmed in advance (how should a self-driving car act if a crash is unavoidable?)

Under the Data Protection Act, people have rights not to be subject to some purely automated decisions with significant effects, and can ask for a human to review them.

1.5.2c

Environmental effects

Harm

  • Energy use: data centres, cryptocurrency mining and training large AI models use huge amounts of electricity and water for cooling
  • Mining rare and precious metals for devices damages the environment and can involve poor working conditions
  • E-waste: devices are replaced often (planned obsolescence, fashion); toxic materials such as lead and mercury can leak from landfill; waste is often shipped to poorer countries

Help

  • Video calls and remote working cut travel
  • Smart systems reduce waste: smart meters, heating and traffic control, efficient route planning
  • Climate and weather modelling; monitoring pollution and wildlife
  • Paperless offices; more efficient modern hardware; recycling schemes
1.5.2d

Censorship, monitoring and analysing personal information

CensorshipControlling what people can access or publish. Some governments block news, social media or political content; schools and companies filter websites; platforms remove harmful content. For: protects children, stops extremism and illegal material. Against: limits free speech and access to information; who decides what is acceptable?; can be used to silence opposition; easily bypassed with VPNs.
Monitoring behaviourEmployers monitoring emails, keystrokes and web use; CCTV with facial recognition; tracking via phones' location. For: security, crime prevention, productivity, safeguarding. Against: invasion of privacy, lack of trust, data could be misused or leaked; people behave differently when watched.
Analysing personal informationOrganisations collect large amounts of data (big data) and use data mining to find patterns: targeted advertising, recommendations, credit scoring, insurance pricing, predicting behaviour, election campaigns. For: personalised services, better medical research, fraud detection. Against: people don't know how much is known about them; profiling and discrimination; manipulation; data breaches.
1.5.2e

Piracy and offensive communications

Piracy is copying and distributing software, music, films or games without permission. The internet makes it easy and hard to trace. It breaks the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act, takes income from creators, and pirated files often carry malware. Responses include licence keys, digital rights management (DRM), online activation, and cheaper legal streaming services.

Offensive communications include trolling, cyberbullying, harassment, threats and hate speech. Anonymity and the speed of sharing make them easy to send and hard to remove. It is an offence to send grossly offensive, indecent or threatening messages (under laws such as the Malicious Communications Act 1988 and Communications Act 2003), and platforms have duties to tackle harmful content. There is tension between protecting people and protecting free speech.

1.5.2f

Layout, colour paradigms and character sets

Software and websites are used by people across the world, so designers must think about different cultures and needs:

  • Layout: some languages are read right to left (Arabic, Hebrew), so the whole layout may need to be mirrored; translated text can be much longer, so layouts must be flexible.
  • Colour paradigms: colours mean different things in different cultures — red means danger or a loss in the West but luck and celebration in China; white is associated with weddings in some cultures and mourning in others. Around 1 in 12 men are colour-blind, so colour shouldn't be the only way information is shown.
  • Character sets: Unicode is needed to display all the world's alphabets and symbols (see 1.4.1). Date formats, currency and units also differ.
  • Accessibility: support screen readers, keyboard navigation, adjustable text size and good contrast for people with disabilities.

In the exam

Extended questions on ethics are marked on knowledge, application to the scenario and evaluation. Consider different stakeholders (users, companies, employees, society), discuss both sides, link to the law where relevant, and finish with a conclusion that follows from your arguments.
