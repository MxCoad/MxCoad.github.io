Computing-related legislation

The four UK laws you need to know: what each one covers, what it allows or forbids, and how to apply it to a scenario. Exam questions almost always describe a situation and ask which law applies and why.

1.5.1a

The Data Protection Act 2018

The Data Protection Act 2018 is the UK's version of the GDPR. It controls how personal data — information about a living, identifiable person — is collected, stored and used.

Data subjectThe person the data is about.
Data controllerThe organisation that decides why and how the data is processed, and is responsible for following the Act.
Data processorAn organisation that processes data on the controller's behalf (e.g. a cloud provider).
ICOThe Information Commissioner's Office enforces the Act. It can investigate, order changes and issue fines of up to £17.5 million or 4% of annual global turnover, whichever is higher.

The data protection principles

Personal data must be:

  1. Processed lawfully, fairly and transparently (there must be a legal basis such as consent, and people must be told what happens to their data)
  2. Collected for specified, explicit purposes and not used for anything else (purpose limitation)
  3. Adequate, relevant and limited to what is needed (data minimisation)
  4. Accurate and kept up to date
  5. Kept no longer than necessary (storage limitation)
  6. Kept secure against unauthorised access, loss or damage (integrity and confidentiality)

And the controller must be able to show it complies (accountability).

Rights of data subjects

People have the right to be informed about how their data is used; to access it (a subject access request, normally free and answered within a month); to have it corrected or erased; to restrict or object to processing; to data portability (take it to another provider); and rights about automated decision-making. Some exemptions apply, e.g. for national security and preventing crime.

1.5.1b

The Computer Misuse Act 1990

The Computer Misuse Act made hacking and spreading malware criminal offences. Each offence builds on the one before:

Section 1Up to 2 years in prisonUnauthorised access to computer material: accessing a system or data without permission, e.g. logging in with someone else's password, or guessing your way into an account, even just to look.
Section 2Up to 5 yearsUnauthorised access with intent to commit or facilitate further offences: hacking in order to commit another crime, e.g. getting into a bank system to steal money, or into an email account to blackmail someone.
Section 3Up to 10 yearsUnauthorised acts with intent to impair the operation of a computer, or recklessly doing so: changing or deleting data, spreading viruses and other malware, denial of service attacks.

Later amendments added offences for making, supplying or obtaining tools for use in these crimes (section 3A) and for acts causing serious damage, e.g. to national infrastructure (section 3ZA, up to life imprisonment).

In the exam

Name the section and say why it applies to the scenario. One action can break more than one section: someone who logs in with a stolen password (s1) and then deletes files (s3).

1.5.1c

The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988

The CDPA protects intellectual property: the creator of an original work owns it and decides who can copy and use it.

  • Covers software (protected as a literary work), music, films, books, images, artwork and broadcasts.
  • Copyright is automatic — it doesn't have to be registered — and for most works lasts 70 years after the creator's death.
  • It is illegal to copy, distribute, adapt, rent or broadcast a work without permission, e.g. installing one licensed copy of software on several machines, sharing films online, or using someone's photos on your website.
  • Some limited use is allowed as fair dealing, e.g. short quotations for criticism, review or private study.
  • Patents protect inventions (how something works), must be applied for, and last up to 20 years. In the UK, a computer program "as such" can't be patented, but an invention that uses software may be.

Software is distributed under a licence, which sets what the user may do: proprietary licences restrict copying and modification; open source licences allow them under conditions (see 1.2.2).

1.5.1d

The Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000

RIPA sets out when public bodies — the police, security services and some others such as HMRC and local councils — may carry out surveillance and intercept communications, and the rules they must follow.

  • Interception of the content of communications (e.g. emails, phone calls) needs a warrant, authorised by the Home Secretary.
  • Authorities can obtain communications data — who contacted whom, when and from where, but not the content — from ISPs and phone companies.
  • ISPs can be required to provide access to customers' communications and to install equipment to make interception possible.
  • People can be required to hand over encryption keys or decrypt data; refusing is an offence.
  • It also covers covert surveillance and the use of undercover sources.
  • It makes it an offence for others to intercept communications without authority.

Supporters say it is needed to fight terrorism and serious crime. Critics say it invades privacy, has been used by councils for minor matters such as checking school catchment areas, and that keeping data on everyone treats everyone as a suspect.

Going further

Many of RIPA's interception powers have since been replaced by the Investigatory Powers Act 2016 (nicknamed the "Snooper's Charter"), which requires ISPs to keep records of the websites customers visit for 12 months. The specification still names RIPA, so learn it — but mentioning the newer Act in a discussion shows wider understanding.

1.5.1e

Applying the laws

Scenario questions often involve more than one law. Read carefully: who did what, to whose data or system, and with what intention?

Try it: which Act?

Match each situation to the law that is most relevant.

Try it: which section of the Computer Misuse Act?

Choose the most serious section each person has broken.

Common mistake

The Data Protection Act is about organisations handling personal data. A hacker breaking into a system breaks the Computer Misuse Act; the company that failed to secure the data may have broken the DPA.
