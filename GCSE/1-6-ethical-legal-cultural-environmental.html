OCR J277 · Paper 1 · Topic 1.6

Ethical, legal, cultural and environmental impacts

How digital technology affects people, society and the planet, and the laws that control how it's used. This topic usually appears in the long, 8-mark "discuss" question on Paper 1, so practise planning balanced answers.

1.6.1

Ethical and privacy issues

Ethical issues are about whether something is morally right or wrong — not just whether it's legal. Different people can reasonably disagree.

  • Jobs and automation — robots and AI can do work faster and more cheaply, but people may lose their jobs. New jobs are also created (e.g. building and maintaining the systems).
  • Bias — AI trained on biased data can make unfair decisions, e.g. about job applications or loans.
  • Responsibility — if a self-driving car crashes, who is to blame: the owner, the manufacturer or the programmer?
  • The digital divide — some people can't access technology because of cost, where they live or a lack of skills, so they miss out on services, education and jobs.
  • Online behaviour — cyberbullying, trolling and the spread of false information.

Privacy

Privacy is about people's right to control information about themselves. Technology collects huge amounts of personal data: location tracking on phones, cookies following you around websites, social media posts, smart speakers listening for commands, CCTV and facial recognition.

There's often a trade-off: personalised services, convenience and security in exchange for giving up some privacy. Risks include data being sold, shared without consent, or stolen in a data breach.

In the exam

For "discuss" questions, give both sides and say who is affected (the stakeholders): users, companies, employees, the government, society. One well-explained point beats three listed ones.

1.6.1

Cultural issues

Cultural issues are about how technology changes the way people live, behave and communicate, and how it affects different groups in society.

  • Communication — social media and messaging let people keep in touch worldwide and build online communities, but can reduce face-to-face contact.
  • Changing industries — streaming has changed how music, films and TV are made and paid for; online shopping has affected high streets.
  • The generation gap — older people may find new technology harder to use and be left out as services move online.
  • Different values — what is acceptable to post or view varies between cultures and countries; some governments censor the Internet.
  • Wellbeing — pressure from social media, screen time and "always on" culture.

Common mistake

Don't mix up cultural and ethical. Cultural: how society and people's way of life change. Ethical: whether it is right or wrong.

1.6.1

Environmental issues

Negative impacts

  • Raw materials — devices contain metals that must be mined, damaging habitats, and some are rare and running out.
  • Energy — manufacturing devices, charging them and running data centres (which also need cooling) use a lot of electricity, often from fossil fuels.
  • E-waste — people replace devices often, so millions are thrown away. They contain toxic materials that can leak from landfill, and e-waste is often shipped to poorer countries.

Positive impacts

  • Video calls and remote working reduce travel.
  • Smart heating and lighting reduce energy use.
  • Computers model climate change and monitor the environment.
  • Digital documents reduce paper use.
  • Devices can be repaired, reused or recycled.

Going further · grades 8–9

Planned obsolescence is when products are designed to stop working well or stop being supported after a few years, so people buy new ones. Laws in some places now give people a "right to repair" — making spare parts and repair manuals available so devices last longer.

1.6.1

Legislation

You need to know three laws and what each one covers.

Data Protection Act

2018 (the UK version of GDPR)

Controls how organisations use personal data. Data must be:

  • used fairly, lawfully and transparently
  • used only for specified purposes
  • adequate, relevant and limited to what's needed
  • accurate and kept up to date
  • kept no longer than necessary
  • kept secure

Computer Misuse Act

1990

Makes hacking and spreading malware illegal. Three offences:

  • Unauthorised access to computer material
  • Unauthorised access with intent to commit further offences (e.g. to steal money)
  • Unauthorised modification of computer material — changing or deleting data, or spreading malware

Copyright, Designs and Patents Act

1988

Protects intellectual property: original work such as music, films, books, images and software.

  • It's illegal to copy, share, modify or sell it without the owner's permission
  • Copyright applies automatically — it doesn't need registering
  • Patents protect inventions

Under the Data Protection Act, people (data subjects) also have rights: to see the data held about them (a subject access request), to have mistakes corrected, and in many cases to have their data deleted.

Common mistake

Downloading a pirated film breaks the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act, not the Computer Misuse Act. The Computer Misuse Act is about unauthorised access to computers.

1.6.1

Software licences

A software licence is a legal agreement that sets out how software can be used and shared.

Open source

  • The source code is available for anyone to see
  • Users can modify it and share their versions
  • Usually free
  • Often supported by a community of volunteers
  • ✗ May have less official support or fewer features
  • Examples: Linux, Firefox, LibreOffice

Proprietary (closed source)

  • The source code is not available
  • Users buy a licence to use it but can't modify or share it
  • Usually costs money
  • The company provides support and updates
  • ✗ Users can't fix problems or add features themselves
  • Examples: Microsoft Office, Adobe Photoshop, most games

Try it: open source or proprietary?

Choose which kind of licence each statement describes.

Common mistake

"Free" doesn't mean open source. Plenty of free apps are proprietary — you can't see or change their code.
